# Abstract: the standalone summary

Written last, once every section is kept, because it summarises them; any earlier abstract text
is revised then. Claim strength and citation checks are in `references/global.md` sections 2 and
4; they bind every sentence here.

## 1. Purpose

- The abstract is often the only part read and the only substantive part indexed in many
  databases, so it must reflect the article's content accurately (ICMJE,
  https://www.icmje.org/recommendations/browse/manuscript-preparation/preparing-for-submission.html).
  It must stand alone: a reader who sees nothing else gets the question, the approach, the main
  numbers and the answer.
- It summarises the whole study. The Introduction opens the question and the Conclusion gives
  the take-home message only (`references/sections/conclusion.md` section 1); the abstract holds
  both ends plus the methods and the results.

## 2. Structure and moves

- **Moves, in order**: context, purpose, method, results, conclusion, which is IMRaD in miniature
  (Hyland's five moves: introduction, purpose, method, product, conclusion,
  https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1076961.pdf , **snippet-level**, also cited as Hyland
  2004). Studies of real abstracts find the introduction and conclusion moves are the ones most
  often missing (same source, **snippet-level**); check that both are present.
- **ICMJE content**: context or background; purpose; basic procedures (selection, settings,
  measurements, analytical methods); main findings with specific effect sizes and their
  significance where possible; principal conclusions; new and important aspects; important
  limitations; no overinterpretation (ICMJE, above).
- **Nature summary paragraph**, a template for an unstructured abstract
  (https://www.nature.com/documents/nature-summary-paragraph.pdf): one or two sentences of basic
  introduction for any scientist; two or three of more detailed background; one sentence stating
  the general problem; one sentence of the main result with "Here we show" or its equivalent; two
  or three on what the result reveals against previous knowledge; one or two placing it in a
  general context; optionally two or three of broader perspective. About 190 words without the
  last part, up to 300 with it.
- **Structured or unstructured: sources differ.** Structured abstracts contain more information
  and read more easily, but are about 35% longer, and the search-speed advantage is not shown on
  real MEDLINE abstracts (Hartley 2004, J Med Libr Assoc 92:368,
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC442180). Of 362 software-engineering abstracts only 29%
  were complete (background, objective, method, result, conclusion); structured ones were about
  twice as likely to be complete (Prechelt et al. 2025, https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.21634). ICMJE
  requires structured abstracts for original research; ML venues use one paragraph. Default:
  follow the target venue's template (section 5); when it allows either, write the unstructured
  paragraph so that each move of the structured version is still present.
- **Reporting checklists.** CONSORT for abstracts (Hopewell et al. 2008, PLoS Med 5:e20,
  https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.0050020 , **snippet-level**) is for randomised trials and
  does not apply. STROBE item 1b asks for "an informative and balanced summary of what was done
  and what was found", and STROBE lists a conference-abstracts checklist
  (https://www.strobe-statement.org/checklists/ ; the item wording is **snippet-level**). Use them
  only as a completeness check by analogy; this paper is neither a trial nor an observational
  study of people.

## 3. Content rules

- **Main numbers with their uncertainty**: the headline rates or effect sizes with intervals or
  the test that backs them, each one the same value the Results report (ICMJE asks for specific
  effect sizes). No number appears that the main text does not contain.
- **No new information.** Abstracts with data that conflicted with the body, or that appeared
  nowhere in it, were 18 to 68% of abstracts across six general medical journals (Pitkin,
  Branagan, Burmeister, JAMA 1999;281:1110, https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.281.12.1110 ; figures from
  secondary summaries, https://chiro.org/ChiroZine/ABSTRACTS/Accuracy_of_Data.shtml ,
  **snippet-level**). Check every abstract sentence against a kept sentence of the body.
- **No citations** (PLOS ONE, https://journals.plos.org/plosone/s/submission-guidelines : "Do not
  include citations in abstracts").
- **No undefined abbreviations**; avoid abbreviations where possible (PLOS ONE, above). An
  abbreviation defined in the abstract is defined again at first use in the body.
- **Method without methodological detail**: say how the study was done, not how each test works
  (PLOS ONE, above).
- **Limitations**: note the important ones (ICMJE), in one clause, not a list.
- **Claims and scope**: the abstract's claims reflect the contributions and scope
  (`references/sections/introduction.md` section 5); the generalisation scope is
  `references/sections/discussion.md` section 6.

## 4. The conclusion sentence and spin

- The abstract's conclusion agrees with the main-text conclusion and with the results; the
  sentence-by-sentence comparison is `references/sections/conclusion.md` section 3.
- Spin evidence and the checks for it are in `references/sections/conclusion.md` section 4. The
  abstract-specific point: abstracts are read apart from the text and spread into other databases
  and media, so a distortion there travels furthest (Winker, editorial accompanying Pitkin 1999,
  **snippet-level**, from the summary above). Run the spin checks on the abstract first.

## 5. Venue notes

- **PLOS ONE**: at most 300 words; objectives, how the study was done without methodological
  detail, the most important results and their significance; no citations; abbreviations avoided
  if possible; also entered in a separate submission field. The fetched guidelines say nothing on
  abstract subheadings, keywords or an author summary
  (https://journals.plos.org/plosone/s/submission-guidelines). Full title at most 250 characters,
  short title at most 100, sentence case, understandable outside the field.
- **IJPDS**: at most 300 words; research articles use the headings Introduction, Objectives,
  Methods, Results (including relevant statistics), Conclusions, in that order; other article
  types may use an unstructured abstract. Keywords from MeSH. Longer manuscripts add 3 to 5
  highlight bullets after the abstract ("longer" is not defined). No plain-language summary
  (https://ijpds.org/index.php/ijpds/author-guidelines).
- **TMLR**: the template limits the abstract to one paragraph
  (https://www.overleaf.com/latex/templates/tmlr-journal-submission/fcjxjtfqrsqb). No word limit
  found; the author guide and editorial policies say nothing on the abstract
  (https://jmlr.org/tmlr/author-guide.html ; https://jmlr.org/tmlr/editorial-policies.html). The
  OpenReview abstract field's length limit is unverified (venues configure it); paste the same
  paragraph there and check the form at submission.
- **EPJ Data Science**: unverified. The guidelines page
  (https://epjdatascience.springeropen.com/submission-guidelines/preparing-your-manuscript)
  redirects to https://link.springer.com/journal/13688/submission-guidelines , which redirects to
  a Springer login. Check the word limit and structure there before submission.
- **Plain-language summary or significance statement**: none of the fetched venue pages requires
  one. TMLR's Broader Impact statement is not a summary (`references/sections/conclusion.md`
  section 5).

## 6. Discoverability

- The title and abstract carry the terms a reader would search for (the study object, the data
  source, the method family), with the main term early in the title and in the first two
  sentences, which are often all a search engine shows (Wiley SEO guidance,
  https://authorservices.wiley.com/asset/photos/promote.html/SEOforAuthorsLINKS.pdf ,
  **snippet-level**; the live Wiley page returned empty). Repeat key terms naturally; do not stuff
  them.
- Keywords add synonyms and related terms not already in the title (same source,
  **snippet-level**; at least five keywords there). Use the venue's vocabulary where it fixes one
  (IJPDS: MeSH).
- Use the settled terms (`references/global.md` section 3): a search term in the abstract that
  the body calls something else is a terminology defect, not a discoverability gain.

## 7. Mistakes to check for

Check the draft for each, and fix or flag:

- a missing context or conclusion move (section 2);
- results without numbers, or numbers without uncertainty;
- a number, claim or term absent from the kept body (section 3);
- a conclusion stronger than the results or different from the main-text conclusion (section 4);
- citations, undefined abbreviations, or methodological detail;
- over the venue's word limit or off its heading structure (section 5);
- search terms missing from the title or the first two sentences (section 6).
